How the peg pyramid works

The board forms a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the one above it. A ball dropped at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at each peg, repeating that split all the way down. After the final row, it lands in one of the pockets at the bottom — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because each path is built from repeated fifty-fifty decisions, balls tend to gather near the centre and only rarely reach the outer edges. Statisticians call this a Galton board, and the pattern it creates follows a binomial distribution. In practical terms, the pay table is the reverse of that curve: common centre pockets pay less, while rare edge pockets pay more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is broadly linked to its probability, with a small house margin trimmed away. When you multiply each payout by its chance of landing, the total comes in slightly under one; that difference is the operator's edge. In most versions, the stated return to player sits somewhere around 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and the selected settings.

A simple check helps keep the game in perspective: no combination of rows and risk changes the long-run maths in your favour. Those settings only move variance around — the size of the swings — not the average result.

Trusted releases use a certified random number generator for every deflection, and provably fair versions publish seed hashes so each round can be checked afterwards. What no version can do is let you steer the ball.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes grow while their chance of landing falls. The overall return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the path, while high risk concentrates value in rare edge hits. The right choice depends on how much movement your budget can handle.
